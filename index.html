It would modestly improve predicted success, but it would do so for exactly the reason that it’s tilting admissions towards more advantaged students. The SAT is successful at predicting because it identifies students’ advantage.1

It essentially turns this measure of background that we wouldn’t want to use directly in admissions — turns it into something that looks like it’s meritocratic but isn’t.

One implication of that is that if you could somehow adjust the SAT to reduce gaps by student background — which, to be clear, UC has never done and could not legally do — but if you could do that, the predictive value of the SAT would fall. The high school GPA’s would go up. So the question isn’t whether the SAT carries any information. Comments

  1. According to UC’s STTF, adding SAT to GPA substantially improves prediction of college grades, even among students with similar levels of socioeconomic advantage. For the subset of students with family incomes under $30,000, adding SAT to GPA nearly doubles the amount of variance explained, from 12% to 22%

  2. Prof. Rothstein defines “student background” quite broadly. It includes not only student attributes such as race and gender but also high school attributes such as Academic Performance Index (API) score. A school’s API score is a function of its students’ standardized test scores. Given school API score, student SAT score necessarily loses significance. API and SAT scores measure roughly the same thing. Meanwhile, GPA necessarily gains significance. That’s because GPAs measure academic performance relative to school-specific averages. Given average academic performance, GPA recovers significance that it loses when removed from the context of grading.

  3. Prof. Rothstein refers here to the following: “without demographic controls the standardized SAT coefficient is 0.38 and the standardized high school GPA coefficient is 0.22. […] With controls, the SAT coefficient falls to 0.34 and the GPA coefficient rises to 0.34” (FAQ p. 15). In short, the SAT and GPA coefficients become equal.

  4. At UC’s selective campuses, admission of URM applicants did not markedly change in 2021. At UCLA, admission of URM applicants has increased steadily for more than a decade. Gains since 2021 have been consistent with this long-term trend. At UC Berkeley, a spike in URM applicants’ relative odds preceded the 2021 cycle by a year. At UC San Diego, a similar spike appears after the 2021, in 2022. Along with San Diego, Santa Barbara and Irvine peaked in 2022 and have since steadily fallen.

  5. Changes in applicant behavior cannot easily account for changes in the composition of UC’s admit pool. As Prof. Rothstein acknowledges in his FAQ, the URM share of UC applicants did not change: “increases were roughly proportional to the overall increase, so the demographic composition of the applicant pool did not change dramatically. The share of applicants who were URM or first generation was stable or fell slightly at all campuses except Merced and Riverside, which saw somewhat larger declines” (p. 4).

  6. Demographic changes in UC’s admit pool are more plausibly explained by a different kind of policy change. School-level selection has changed more than student-level selection. Conditional on high school, admit-rate gaps between URM and non-URM applicants have largely persisted. What has changed: between-school differences. Admit rates have increased at high schools with higher concentrations of disadvantaged students.

  7. A reader of UC’s 2020 STTF Report could reasonably conclude that SAT scores had little if any influence on UC’s diversity around 2020. The report’s tables of SAT and GPA distributions are consistent with a process that directly selected only on GPA and selected on SAT score only indirectly, through its correlation with GPA. To account for URM applicants’ higher odds of admission ceteris paribus, a plausible model of the selection process would need to include bonus points for first-generation and low-income applicants.

    The expected effect of removing SAT scores would be nil.

  8. SAT scores predict academic outcomes. Selecting on a predictor of academic outcomes can be reasonably expected to affect academic outcomes.

  9. These students were required to submit SAT/ACT scores when they applied to UC.

  10. At Berkeley, URM applicants’ normalized odds of admission increased from 0.79 in 2019 to 1.28 in 2020. Since UC became test-free, URM applicants’ normalized odds have fallen, to 1.18 in 2021 and 1.06 in 2025.

  11. As Prof. Rothstein notes in his FAQ: “the first-generation share fell at Santa Cruz and (in 2022 and thereafter) Irvine, and was roughly stable at Davis and Santa Barbara” (p. 5).
